Why we stopped asking for timesheets on Friday

Reconstructing a week from memory produces fiction. Here is what replaced the Friday reminder at the studios we work with.

Elena Voss, Head of Product · Sep 18, 2026 · 6 min read

Every studio has the same ritual. At four on Friday a reminder goes out, and twenty people try to remember what they did on Tuesday. Some open their calendar, some scroll their commits, most guess. The result is a timesheet that looks precise and is mostly fiction.

The cost is not the twenty minutes. It is the hours that never make it onto an invoice, the budget that looks healthy until it suddenly isn't, and the quiet resentment of people who became designers and engineers to do the work, not to account for it.

The work already leaves a trail

Nearly everything a studio does is recorded somewhere. Meetings live in calendars. Code lives in commits. Design work lives in version histories. Calls have start and end times. The information needed for an honest timesheet exists before anyone opens one.

So we stopped asking people to write their week and started asking them to confirm it. Notch drafts each day from those sources, matches entries to projects, and marks anything it is unsure about. Confirming a week takes about a minute.

People are good at recognising what they did. They are bad at recalling it from nothing.

What changed for the studios

  • Captured billable time rose by a median of 9% in the first month, mostly from short calls and review sessions that used to vanish.
  • Month-end closed a week earlier, because timesheets were approved as the month went, not at the end of it.
  • Nobody missed the Friday reminder.

Drafted timesheets are not about watching people more closely. Notch only reads the sources a person connects, and everything stays a suggestion until they confirm it. The goal is fewer guesses, not more surveillance.
